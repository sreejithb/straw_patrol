Why the Synthetic Data Failed

A comparison of our generated comments with real comments, and what we must change.

1. The question

Our model finds fallacies well in text that our generator writes. It finds them poorly in comments that people write.

On our own long, realistic-style test set, run I scored 84%. On CoCoLoFa and MAFALDA (text from people), it scored about 30%.

We asked: what is different about the two kinds of text? We measured the texts, trained simple classifiers, and read samples side by side.

Where the data comes from. "Synthetic" means the 2,738 fallacy and 585 "None" examples from the realistic data round. "CoCoLoFa" means 610 top-level comments from its test file (crowd workers wrote them with an LLM to help). "MAFALDA" means 196 real texts from Reddit and political debates. The numbers below come from a script run on these files.

2. Test 1: can a simple classifier tell them apart?

We trained a bag-of-words classifier. It sees only the words, not the meaning. We asked it to separate our synthetic comments from the real ones.

If the texts were alike, the classifier would score 50%. It scored much higher.

This means our synthetic text has a strong "accent". A model can learn this accent. When it does, it learns what generated text looks like, not what a fallacy is.

The third bar is a second warning. In our own data, the wording alone tells a fallacy from a "None" example 89% of the time. Section 5 explains why this is dangerous.

3. Test 2: what is different?

The topics are different

Our topic list had "politics" and "law". But the generator made every topic small and friendly: a pasta place, an old mill, a neighbor's dog. Real comments are about Hong Kong, Myanmar, courts and elections.

Our comments are never excited. They never use "!". They rarely ask a rhetorical question. They often start with the same polite words: "I get that… but honestly…". They use @-replies far more than real comments do, because our instructions asked for that format.

Real comments are blunter. Some are very long. Some use slang and insults.

The fallacies are different

Our fallacies are complete and easy to see. Real ones are partial and vague.

Slippery slope, our synthetic example:

"If the city lets officers skip one warrant in a 'minor' case, then shortcuts become normal, then searches get sloppy, and eventually rights mean whatever the department says they mean."

Slippery slope, a CoCoLoFa example:

"Does anyone else think we're going too far with making everything a joke and a meme? … Where will it end? … Eventually, nobody will take anything seriously and we'll all suffer from poor governments…"

The first one shows the whole chain, step by step. The second one has only a question and a vague end point. A model that only saw the first kind will miss the second kind.

5. The "None" problem

This is the most important finding for the false alarms.

In our data, the "None" examples are written in a polite, balanced voice. The fallacy examples are written in a more one-sided voice. The wording shows this:

A classifier that uses only these words separates our two classes with 89% accuracy. But it fails on real text. It says "None" for 13% of real CoCoLoFa "None" comments, and for 8% of real CoCoLoFa fallacy comments. It cannot tell them apart.

This agrees with run I. We added more polite "None" examples. The "None" recall on CoCoLoFa test went down, from 40% to 25%. The model learned that polite text means "None". Real "None" comments are not polite.

Some real "None" labels are also doubtful. The example in the figure is arguably an appeal to consequences. So part of the problem is in the labels, but the tone shortcut is a real problem in our data.

6. What we got wrong

A correction. After run I, we said that text style was not the problem, because the model scored 84% on our own realistic test set and did not improve on real text. That conclusion was wrong.

The realistic round only changed the length and the format. It did not change the topics, the voice, the strength of the fallacies, or the link between tone and label. The 84% score measures how well the model reads our generator. It does not measure real skill.

7. How we fix the generator

  1. Use news as the setting. Give the generator a realistic news headline and source for each comment. Include real institutions and places. Cover politics, world affairs, law and health, not only everyday scenes.
  2. Use many voices. Ban the common opening phrases ("I get that", "people keep saying", "honestly"). Ask for short, angry, formal, sarcastic and sloppy styles. Include "!", rhetorical questions, typos, slang and insults at realistic rates. Allow lengths up to about 150 words.
  3. Vary the strength of the fallacy. Mix clear, implicit and partial fallacies. Put the fallacy in one sentence of a longer comment. Stop using the same "then… then… eventually" chain.
  4. Break the link between tone and label. Write assertive "None" comments that give reasons. Write polite, hedged comments that do contain a fallacy. Make matched pairs in the same voice.
  5. Use the classifiers as gates. After each batch, run the same tests as in Figure 1. We accept a batch only if the synthetic-versus-real classifier falls below about 70%, and the fallacy-versus-"None" classifier falls close to 50%.
  6. Get real labels. Label about 100 real comments by hand. Only human labels can tell us how good the model is on real text.

What we do not know

  • CoCoLoFa comments were written with an LLM as a helper, so they are not fully natural text. MAFALDA is real text, but it is small (196 texts).
  • The classifiers use simple word counts. A stronger test (for example, an LLM that judges a blind mix of texts) could find more differences.
  • We did not measure the "@-reply or quote" rate in MAFALDA reliably.
  • Fixing the generator may not fix everything. The model is also small, and the external labels are noisy. We will know more after a human-labeled test set exists.
